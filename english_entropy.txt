Entropy: From Thermodynamics to Life

Among the many concepts in physics, few are as profound and abstract—yet simultaneously so relevant to our daily lives—as "entropy." A hot cup of coffee eventually cools down; an ice cube in a glass quietly melts; a tidy desk becomes cluttered with odds and ends after just a few days; a drop of ink spreads through clear water until it is evenly dispersed. We take these occurrences for granted, rarely asking: why do they always unfold in one direction, never the reverse? The answer lies within this single, small term: "entropy."

The story of entropy begins in the nineteenth century with the steam engine. As the Industrial Revolution swept across Europe, engineers sought to build more efficient heat engines—only to discover that, no matter how much they improved the design, a portion of the heat was always lost, failing to be converted into useful work. The French engineer Sadi Carnot was the first to contemplate this issue, noting that there is an upper limit to the efficiency of any heat engine. Later, the German physicist Rudolf Clausius formulated the second law of thermodynamics and, in 1865, coined the term "entropy." He famously remarked, in essence, that while the total energy of the universe remains constant, its entropy tends toward a maximum value.

At that time, however, Clausius’s entropy remained a purely macroscopic concept; its underlying nature was not yet understood. It was the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann who finally unlocked the mystery. Approaching the subject from the microscopic realm, he linked entropy to the arrangement of molecules: the greater the number of microscopic states corresponding to a given macroscopic state, the higher the entropy. A deck of playing cards offers a simple analogy: there is only one way to arrange a deck by suit and rank, whereas there is an astronomical number of ways for the cards to be arranged once they are shuffled. Thus, a shuffled deck will almost never spontaneously return to an ordered state—not because of some mysterious prohibition, but simply because the possibilities for disorder are overwhelmingly vast. Boltzmann’s entropy formula is even inscribed on his tombstone, a testament to the significance of his discovery.

In this light, the second law of thermodynamics is, at its core, a statistical law. It is not that a decrease in entropy is strictly impossible, but rather that in a system containing a vast number of particles, the probability of such a decrease is so infinitesimal that it can be safely ignored. The chance of all the molecules in a glass of water—numbering in the quintillions—spontaneously moving in the same direction, causing the water to boil, is even slimmer than the chance of a monkey randomly striking keys on a typewriter to produce the entire novel *Dream of the Red Chamber*. It is no wonder that the British astronomer Arthur Eddington remarked that if your theory contradicts the second law of thermodynamics, there is no hope for it; you must simply concede defeat.

Physicists once conceived of a "demon" to challenge this law. "Maxwell’s Demon" could distinguish between fast and slow molecules and operate a small gate to separate them, seemingly reducing entropy without any expenditure of effort. It was later understood, however, that the demon must inevitably pay a price when acquiring or erasing information; once this cost is factored in, the total entropy does not decrease. This thought experiment also established a profound link between entropy and information.

In the twentieth century, the scope of entropy expanded significantly. In 1948, while studying communication, the American mathematician Claude Shannon discovered that the uncertainty of information could be quantified using a formula analogous to the one for entropy in statistical mechanics—a concept he termed "information entropy." In this sense, the more unexpected a message is, the more information it carries; a message that is entirely predictable contains zero information. This insight laid the foundation for modern communication and data compression; the mobile phones, the internet, and the various compression formats we use today are all rooted in the concept of information entropy. In the era of machine learning, "cross-entropy" has become one of the most widely used loss functions for training models.

Entropy increase is often misinterpreted as a fatalistic, pessimistic conclusion—as if everything is destined to descend into decay and chaos. Yet, the very existence of life reveals another possibility. As the physicist Erwin Schrödinger pointed out in his book *What Is Life?*, life sustains itself by feeding on "negative entropy." A plant absorbs low-entropy sunlight, synthesizing disordered carbon dioxide and water into intricate organic molecules; a human being consumes organized food and expels disordered waste heat and matter, thereby maintaining a highly ordered internal structure. This does not violate the second law of thermodynamics, because life functions as an open system: while it creates order locally, it releases even more entropy into the surrounding environment, ensuring that the total entropy of the universe continues to increase. In other words, order can exist—but it always comes at a price.

When we cast our gaze toward the cosmos, the significance of entropy becomes even more profound. Many physicists argue that the "arrow of time"—our ability to distinguish the past from the future—stems from the increase in entropy. Most fundamental physical laws are symmetric under time reversal; a video of billiard balls colliding, when played in reverse, still appears perfectly plausible. Conversely, seeing a shattered glass spontaneously reassemble would strike us as impossible. The distinction lies in the fact that the universe began in a state of extremely low entropy and has been evolving toward higher entropy ever since; what we perceive as the "flow of time" is simply a manifestation of this process. Why the universe started with such low entropy remains an unsolved mystery.

Extrapolating this trend of increasing entropy leads to a chilling hypothesis: the "heat death" of the universe. In the distant future, as stars burn out and temperatures reach a state of total uniformity, there will no longer be any energy gradients capable of performing work—and all change will cease. However, this remains a speculative scenario based on current theories. Issues such as the accelerating expansion of the universe, the nature of dark energy, and the entropy of black holes continue to be subjects of intense debate among physicists; we are far from reaching a definitive conclusion about the ultimate fate of the universe. Regardless, contemplating entropy has allowed humanity to approach the ancient question—"what will be the ultimate end of all things?"—through a scientific lens.

In our daily lives, the implications of entropy are equally significant. A room left untended becomes cluttered; knowledge not reviewed is forgotten; friendships not nurtured drift apart; a body not exercised falls into decline—all of these are reflections of entropy at work. To maintain order, one must continuously invest energy and effort. However, this is not a disheartening conclusion; rather, it serves as a reminder: order is never a free gift—it is an achievement earned, bit by bit, through action. Every act of organizing, every instance of learning, and every thoughtful creation serves as a localized countermeasure against the increase of entropy, adding a measure of order and meaning to the world.

In this way, entropy weaves together the roar of a steam engine, the shuffling of a deck of cards, the transmission of signals, the breath of living beings, and the ultimate fate of the universe—all along a single, unifying thread. It tells us that the evolution of the world has a direction, and that human effort possesses real value. While the Second Law of Thermodynamics may have already dictated the final destination, how one chooses to live—with order and brilliance—before reaching that end remains a question for each individual to answer.
